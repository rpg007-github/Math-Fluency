Math Fluency Primer

The arithmetic and pre-algebra you were never drilled on, explained clearly and honestly, so it stops slowing you down in science courses.

Why You Have These Gaps

If you are reading this because math slows you down in chemistry, biology, or physics, the first thing you need to understand is that the problem almost certainly did not start with you. It started with decisions made in your K-12 education that prioritized “conceptual understanding” over fluency, and in doing so, left a generation of students who understand what multiplication means but cannot do it automatically.

Here is what happened. Starting in the late 1990s and accelerating through the 2000s and 2010s, math education reformers shifted emphasis away from memorization and procedural fluency toward discovery learning and conceptual reasoning. The theory was sound: understanding why something works is better than just knowing how to do it. The execution was catastrophic. You got the theory without the practice. You got the concept without the automaticity. And when you sit down to balance a chemistry equation or calculate a drug dosage, your brain is burning energy on the arithmetic instead of the actual problem.

Then COVID hit. If you were in middle school during the shutdowns, you lost somewhere between one and two years of foundational math instruction at exactly the moment when the building blocks were being laid. Nobody came back and filled that in.

This is not a math guide for people who are bad at math. It is a math guide for people who were not taught math the way it needed to be taught. There is a meaningful difference. Bad at math is a fixed trait. Undertaught is a temporary condition with a straightforward remedy: practice the things you were not made to practice.

What This Guide Covers

These are not advanced topics. They are the foundational skills that show up constantly in college science courses, and that trip up students not because they are hard but because they were never made automatic. Working through this guide will not take long, and the payoff in your science courses will be immediate and significant.

Adding, subtracting, multiplying, and dividing fractions without a calculator. You need these constantly in stoichiometry, dilution problems, and dosage calculations. Most students can do them slowly and painfully; this guide will make them fast.

Solving for a variable, rearranging formulas, and working with ratios and proportions. Nearly every quantitative science problem requires you to do at least one of these things. They are not hard once they are automatic.

How to use this guide: Read each section once to understand the concept and rules. Then work through the examples with pencil and paper, not just by reading. Then use the quiz and flashcards to test yourself until the operations feel automatic. The goal is not just understanding. The goal is speed and confidence, because those are what will actually help you in an exam.

A Note on Calculators

Calculators are useful tools, and you should absolutely use them when they are permitted. This guide is not anti-calculator. But there are three problems with relying on a calculator for everything.

First, many exams in introductory science courses are calculator-free or calculator-limited. If you cannot do the arithmetic by hand, you are in trouble on those exams regardless of how well you understood the concepts.

Second, a calculator cannot tell you whether your answer is reasonable. If you set up a stoichiometry problem wrong and get an answer of 4,500 grams of product from 2 grams of reactant, a calculator will happily confirm that wrong answer. Mental number sense, the ability to estimate whether an answer is in the right ballpark, can only be built through practice with actual numbers.

Third, and most importantly: if you are reaching for a calculator every time you encounter a fraction or a multiplication problem, you are interrupting your own thinking. The cognitive cost of that interruption compounds across an entire exam. Students who have fluent arithmetic can stay in the problem. Students who do not lose the thread every time a number appears.

The goal is automaticity, not memorization for its own sake. You want the mechanics to become so familiar that they require no conscious effort, leaving your full attention free for the actual science. That is what fluency means. It is the same reason skilled readers do not sound out every word: they have automated the decoding so they can focus entirely on meaning.

Fractions

Fractions appear constantly in science: concentrations, ratios, dilutions, stoichiometric coefficients, half-lives, and probability all involve fractional reasoning. The rules are simple and do not change. Once you know them and have practiced them, fraction problems stop being obstacles.

Why so many students struggle with fractions: Because K-12 math instruction spent years having students represent fractions with pictures and manipulatives, which builds intuition, but insufficient time drilling the actual procedures until they became automatic. You probably understand what a fraction represents. The gap is in executing the operations quickly and correctly without thinking hard about them.

The Vocabulary First

Numerator
The top number. How many parts you have.
Denominator
The bottom number. How many parts make a whole.
Reciprocal
Flip the fraction. The reciprocal of 3/4 is 4/3. The reciprocal of 5 is 1/5. You use this every time you divide by a fraction.
Simplifying (Reducing)
Dividing both numerator and denominator by the same number until no common factor remains. 6/8 simplifies to 3/4 because both are divisible by 2.

Rule 1: Multiplying Fractions

Multiply straight across: numerator times numerator, denominator times denominator. This is the easiest fraction operation.

(a/b) × (c/d) = (a × c) / (b × d)
Worked Example
Calculate: (2/3) × (4/5)
(2/3) × (4/5)start
= (2 × 4) / (3 × 5)multiply across
= 8/15already simplified
Answer: 8/15
Worked Example: Simplify First (Saves Time)
Calculate: (3/8) × (4/9)
(3/8) × (4/9)look for common factors first
= (3/9) × (4/8)regroup to see: 3/9 = 1/3, 4/8 = 1/2
= (1/3) × (1/2)cancel before multiplying
= 1/6much cleaner than 12/72 then reducing
Answer: 1/6

Rule 2: Dividing Fractions

Flip the second fraction and multiply. “Dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal” is the rule, and it always works.

(a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c)
Worked Example
Calculate: (5/6) ÷ (2/3)
(5/6) ÷ (2/3)start
= (5/6) × (3/2)flip second fraction and multiply
= 15/12multiply across
= 5/4simplify: divide both by 3
Answer: 5/4 (or 1 and 1/4)

Rule 3: Adding and Subtracting Fractions

You must have the same denominator before you can add or subtract. If the denominators match, add the numerators. If they do not match, find the least common denominator first.

Same denominator: (a/c) + (b/c) = (a + b) / c
Different denominators: get a common denominator first
Worked Example: Same Denominator
Calculate: (3/8) + (1/8)
(3/8) + (1/8)same denominator
= (3 + 1) / 8add numerators only
= 4/8 = 1/2simplify
Answer: 1/2
Worked Example: Different Denominators
Calculate: (1/4) + (2/3)
(1/4) + (2/3)different denominators: need LCD
LCD of 4 and 3 = 12smallest number both divide into
= (3/12) + (8/12)convert: 1/4 × 3/3, 2/3 × 4/4
= 11/12add numerators
Answer: 11/12

Fractions in Science: The Real Reason This Matters

In chemistry, you will calculate concentrations like 0.25 mol/L, which is just 1/4 mol per liter. In biology, you will dilute solutions by factors like 1/10 or 1/100. In physics, you will work with ratios of forces or velocities. In every case, the underlying operation is fraction arithmetic. The students who find these calculations effortless have automated the mechanics. The students who find them stressful are doing fraction arithmetic consciously at the same time they are trying to think about the science.

Quick check on yourself: Without a calculator, what is (3/4) ÷ (1/2)? If you got 3/2 immediately by flipping and multiplying, you are in good shape. If you had to stop and think for more than a few seconds, the flashcards and quiz in this guide will help you build that speed.

Number Facts and Arithmetic Automaticity

This is the section many students feel is beneath them. It is not. Working memory is a limited resource, and every computation you do slowly drains it. The goal here is not to teach you what multiplication is. The goal is to make it fast enough that it requires essentially no effort.

What happened in your math education: Somewhere in the 2000s, many school districts reduced or eliminated timed multiplication drills because educators worried they caused math anxiety. Some did. But the solution to anxiety-producing drills was not to skip fluency practice entirely; it was to find better ways to build it. Many districts did not find better ways. They just stopped. If you are in your late teens or early twenties right now, you were likely in a classroom that believed a calculator made memorized facts unnecessary. The calculator does not help you on a timed exam, and it cannot help you estimate whether your answer is reasonable.

Most students have no problem with the 1s, 2s, 5s, and 10s. The ones that slow people down are the 6s through 9s. Here they are, grouped so the harder ones stand out.

The 9s shortcut: For any single-digit number n multiplied by 9, the answer has digits that add to 9, and the tens digit is always (n−1). So 9 × 7: tens digit = 6 (one less than 7), ones digit = 3 (since 6+3=9), answer = 63. This works for all single-digit multiplications by 9.

Estimation: The Skill That Catches Mistakes

Estimation is the ability to quickly judge whether an answer is in the right ballpark before committing to it. Students who were never made to practice mental math often completely skip estimation, which means they miss obvious errors.

Estimation in Practice
You calculate the molar mass of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and get 1800 g/mol. Is this reasonable?
C: 6 atoms × ~12 g/mol = ~72quick mental estimate
H: 12 atoms × ~1 g/mol = ~12quick mental estimate
O: 6 atoms × ~16 g/mol = ~96quick mental estimate
Total ≈ 72 + 12 + 96 = 180 g/molestimate says ~180
1800 is off by a factor of 10. Something went wrong. Correct answer: 180 g/mol.
Build estimation as a habit, not an afterthought. Before you calculate, ask yourself: what order of magnitude should this answer be? Dozens? Hundreds? Millionths? After you calculate, check whether you landed in that range. This takes two seconds and catches the majority of setup and arithmetic errors.

Algebra Basics for Science Students

Nearly every quantitative problem in introductory science requires you to isolate a variable, rearrange a formula, or set up a proportion. These are not advanced algebra skills. They are basic manipulations that become effortless with practice and remain frustratingly slow without it.

What algebra actually is: A set of rules for maintaining balance. An equation is a statement that two things are equal. Every operation you do to one side, you do to the other side. That is the entire foundation. Everything else follows from that.

The Golden Rule of Algebra

Whatever you do to one side of an equation, you must do to the other side.

This rule never changes. If you add 5 to the left side, you add 5 to the right. If you divide the left side by 3, you divide the right side by 3. Keep this rule in mind and you will never make an illegal algebraic move.

Solving for a Variable: Step by Step

Worked Example: One-Step
Solve for x: 3x = 24
3x = 24start
3x/3 = 24/3divide both sides by 3
x = 8done
x = 8
Worked Example: Two-Step
Solve for x: 2x + 7 = 19
2x + 7 = 19start
2x = 12subtract 7 from both sides
x = 6divide both sides by 2
x = 6

Rearranging Formulas (The Most Important Science Skill)

In science you are given one equation and asked to solve for different variables depending on what is known. The formula does not change. You rearrange it to isolate what you need. This is the single most important algebraic skill for science students, and it is just repeated application of the golden rule.

Worked Example: Ideal Gas Law Rearrangement
The ideal gas law is PV = nRT. Solve for T (temperature).
PV = nRTstart: T is multiplied by n and R
PV / (nR) = Tdivide both sides by nR
T = PV / (nR) — divide the known quantities to find temperature
Worked Example: Molarity Formula
Molarity M = moles / liters. You know M and liters, solve for moles.
M = moles / litersstart
M × liters = molesmultiply both sides by liters
moles = M × liters

Proportions and Cross-Multiplication

A proportion says two ratios are equal: a/b = c/d. Cross-multiplying (a × d = b × c) lets you solve for any one missing value when the other three are known. This appears constantly in stoichiometry, dilution problems, and dosage calculations.

Worked Example: Proportion Setup
If 2 moles of H₂ reacts with 1 mole of O₂, how many moles of O₂ are needed for 5 moles of H₂?
2 mol H₂ / 1 mol O₂ = 5 mol H₂ / x mol O₂set up proportion
2x = 5 × 1cross-multiply
x = 2.5divide both sides by 2
2.5 moles of O₂ are needed

Order of Operations: PEMDAS

You have seen PEMDAS before. The reason it matters in science is that formulas often have multiple operations stacked together, and evaluating them in the wrong order produces wrong answers. Parentheses first, then exponents, then multiplication and division (left to right), then addition and subtraction (left to right).

Worked Example: Order of Operations Matters
Evaluate: 3 + 4 × 2² − 6 ÷ 3
= 3 + 4 × 4 − 6 ÷ 3exponents first: 2² = 4
= 3 + 16 − 2multiplication and division: left to right
= 17addition and subtraction: left to right
Answer: 17 (common wrong answer: 11, from doing addition first)
When in doubt, add parentheses. If you are unsure which part of an expression to evaluate first, write in parentheses around the parts you intend to do first. This makes your intended order explicit and makes errors much easier to catch. Your instructor will not penalize you for extra parentheses. They will penalize you for wrong answers.

Units and Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis is the most powerful quantitative tool in introductory science, and it is dramatically underused because many students were never explicitly taught it. The core idea is elegant: units behave like numbers. You can multiply, divide, and cancel them. If you set up a calculation so the units cancel correctly, the arithmetic will give you the right answer even when you do not remember the formula.

If the units work out, there is a good chance the calculation is set up correctly.
If the units do not work out, the calculation is definitely set up incorrectly.

The Method: Conversion Factors

A conversion factor is a fraction equal to 1, written so the unit you want to cancel is on the opposite side from where it appears in your starting value. Multiplying by a fraction equal to 1 does not change the value, only the units.

Conversion Factor
A ratio of equivalent quantities in different units. Example: (1000 mL / 1 L) or (1 L / 1000 mL). These are both equal to 1, and you choose which form to use based on which unit you want to cancel.
Worked Example: Single Conversion
Convert 2.5 liters to milliliters.
2.5 L × (1000 mL / 1 L)place L in denominator to cancel
= 2.5 × 1000 mLL cancels, mL remains
= 2500 mLdone
2500 mL
Worked Example: Chain Conversion
Convert 60 miles per hour to meters per second. (1 mile = 1609 m, 1 hour = 3600 s)
60 mi/hr × (1609 m / 1 mi) × (1 hr / 3600 s)chain two factors
= (60 × 1609) / 3600 m/smiles cancel, hours cancel
≈ 96,540 / 3600 m/sarithmetic
≈ 26.8 m/sfinal answer
60 mph ≈ 26.8 m/s

Science operates almost entirely in metric. These prefixes are the backbone of all unit conversions in chemistry, biology, and physics. You do not need to memorize the full SI prefix table, but you must know these cold.

The staircase trick for metric conversions: Each step up the prefix ladder multiplies by 10. Each step down divides by 10. From milli- to the base unit is 3 steps up (× 1000). From the base unit to micro- is 3 steps down (÷ 1000 or × 10⁻³). Count the steps and adjust the decimal point.

Catching Errors with Units

The single best habit you can build in science problem-solving is to always write the units with every number, at every step, and check that they cancel correctly before you do any arithmetic. If you have set up the conversion factors correctly, the units that should not be in your answer will cancel and the units that should remain will stay. If you reach the end and the units are wrong, stop before trusting the number.

Practice: Unit Analysis Check

01You want to find grams from moles. You know molar mass in g/mol. How do you set up the multiplication so grams remain and moles cancel?
02You have a speed in km/hr and want m/s. What two conversion factors do you need, and which way does each one go?
03A dosage is expressed as 5 mg/kg of body weight. A patient weighs 70 kg. Write out the unit analysis to find the dose in mg.

Scientific Notation

Science deals with numbers that are inconveniently large or small. The mass of a proton is 0.00000000000000000000000000167 kg. Avogadro’s number is 602,214,076,000,000,000,000,000. Writing these out in full is impractical and error-prone. Scientific notation is the solution: a compact, standardized way to express any number as a coefficient times a power of ten.

N × 10ˣ   where   1 ≤ N < 10

The coefficient N is always a number between 1 and 10 (including 1 but not including 10). The exponent x tells you how many places the decimal point moved.

Converting To Scientific Notation

Worked Example: Large Number
Write 4,500,000 in scientific notation.
4,500,000move decimal left until one digit before it
4.5moved decimal 6 places left
= 4.5 × 10⁶positive exponent for large numbers
4.5 × 10⁶
Worked Example: Small Number
Write 0.000072 in scientific notation.
0.000072move decimal right until one non-zero digit before it
7.2moved decimal 5 places right
= 7.2 × 10⁻⁵negative exponent for small numbers
7.2 × 10⁻⁵

Multiplying in Scientific Notation

Multiply the coefficients. Add the exponents. Adjust if the coefficient is not between 1 and 10.

(A × 10ˣ) × (B × 10ʸ) = (A × B) × 10^(x+y)
Worked Example
Calculate: (3.0 × 10⁴) × (2.0 × 10³)
= (3.0 × 2.0) × 10^(4+3)multiply coefficients, add exponents
= 6.0 × 10⁷done, coefficient is between 1 and 10
6.0 × 10⁷
Worked Example: Adjust After
Calculate: (5.0 × 10⁶) × (4.0 × 10³)
= (5.0 × 4.0) × 10^(6+3)multiply, add exponents
= 20.0 × 10⁹but 20.0 is not between 1 and 10
= 2.0 × 10¹⁰move decimal left one place, add 1 to exponent
2.0 × 10¹⁰

Dividing in Scientific Notation

Divide the coefficients. Subtract the exponents. Adjust if needed.

(A × 10ˣ) ÷ (B × 10ʸ) = (A ÷ B) × 10^(x−y)
Worked Example
Calculate: (8.4 × 10⁸) ÷ (2.1 × 10³)
= (8.4 ÷ 2.1) × 10^(8−3)divide coefficients, subtract exponents
= 4.0 × 10⁵done
4.0 × 10⁵

Adding and Subtracting in Scientific Notation

This is the one that catches students. You cannot add numbers in scientific notation unless the exponents match first. Convert to the same power of ten, then add or subtract the coefficients.

Worked Example
Calculate: (3.2 × 10⁵) + (4.6 × 10⁴)
= (3.2 × 10⁵) + (0.46 × 10⁵)convert 4.6 × 10⁴ to same exponent
= (3.2 + 0.46) × 10⁵now add coefficients
= 3.66 × 10⁵done
3.66 × 10⁵
Why this matters in chemistry: Avogadro’s number (6.022 × 10²³), the charge on an electron (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C), the mass of a hydrogen atom (1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg): you will use these numbers in calculations. If scientific notation arithmetic is slow or uncertain, every calculation involving these constants will take longer and introduce more chances for error.

Significant Figures: A Brief, Practical Guide

Your science instructors care about significant figures because they communicate the precision of a measurement. The rules are not complicated, but students often apply them inconsistently because they were memorized without understanding why they exist.

RuleExampleSig Figs
All non-zero digits count4533
Zeros between non-zeros count4,5034
Leading zeros do not count0.00452
Trailing zeros after decimal count4.5004
Trailing zeros without decimal are ambiguous45002, 3, or 4
Practical rule for calculations: When multiplying or dividing, your answer has as many significant figures as the least precise measurement you used. When adding or subtracting, your answer has the same number of decimal places as the least precise measurement. When in doubt on an exam, keeping three significant figures is rarely wrong.
